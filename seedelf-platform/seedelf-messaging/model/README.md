# The session model (54b)

A symbolic model of [protocol.md](../docs/protocol.md) §3 to §6 and §19, as built (§16), in [Tamarin](https://tamarin-prover.com) 1.12.0: first contact with the static DH, the Double Ratchet with skipped keys, the reader's rule on a cid, continuations, and the verified reveal. [session.spthy](session.spthy) is the model and [check.sh](check.sh) proves it. Chunk 54's 54b.

Two smaller models sit beside it. [envelope.spthy](envelope.spthy) puts the outer envelope around the messages: Phase 1's v1 envelope ([construction.md](../docs/construction.md) §4) alone, then first contact sealed to the reader's identity re-randomized by the message's outer scalar (protocol.md §4.1, §16.2), and the reader's checks of every point its key touches: the envelope's generator and ephemeral point, and F's base and keys proofs (*The outer envelope*). [unlinkability.spthy](unlinkability.spthy) proves the bare envelope unlinkable by observational equivalence, and states the same of a reader's answers to first messages (*Unlinkability*).

It checks the design, not the Rust: nothing here reads the crate. The vectors and the adversarial tests hold the code to §16, and the mutation pass did for the rules before call 16 ([security.md](../docs/security.md), *The session layer*); this holds §16 to its claims.

```bash
./check.sh               # every lemma and pair, then each configuration's wellformedness checks
./check.sh m1_secret     # some runs, by name; a word that names nothing stops it
./check.sh FIRST         # one configuration's runs, by its flag
MODELS=envelope.spthy ./check.sh   # one model
LOGS=/tmp/m ./check.sh   # keep the logs, as <model>-<flag>-<name>.log
```

It needs Docker. The first run builds the image from [Dockerfile](Dockerfile): Tamarin 1.12.0 and Maude 3.5.1, each pinned by its release's SHA-256, on a pinned Ubuntu 24.04. Every lemma then runs in its own container with no network and a memory cap (`CAP`), `PAR` at a time.

**It's heavy.** A lemma takes from seconds to 36 minutes, but `responder_keys2_secret` takes about two hours (6,844 s on 2026-11-22) at 6.4 GB of GHC's maximum residency, 15 GB in use under its 20 GB cap; `skipped_agreement` peaks at 3.4 GB live, six others between 2 and 2.4 GB, and the rest under 2 GB (each run's peak by check.sh's own measure). The whole run took an hour and 56 minutes at the defaults: run it when the machine is otherwise idle. Each run gets `CAP` of memory with no swap and `CPUS` cores, `PAR` at a time, so the defaults (4, 1 core, 8 GB) take at most 32 GB and 4 cores, or 44 GB while the one lemma that asks for 20 GB runs, and `PAR=1` one run's 8 GB and a core. Tamarin's runtime would take every core otherwise, and its heap is held under `CAP`, where GHC compacts in place instead of copying. Compacting is slow, so a run near its cap slows down rather than fails; a run with no result says why it stopped, out of time or of memory, and its peak live heap. Before call 16 the envelope model's runs took 23 minutes of one core in all, none over 2.2 GB in use, and the unlinkability pairs a minute and a GB each. `answers_alike` (call 16) gets 12 GB and two hours from its own `// check.sh diff:` line, and runs last.

check.sh proved every lemma above, and each configuration's wellformedness checks passed (2026-11-22), but for what the gate of 1.10.0 changed, which is to be run: `m3_secret_after_a_genuine_reply` and `responder_state_then_r2_reached` are new, `executable`, `skipped_in_order` and the two `continues_shown_*` traces now name KeyOn, and `I_Read_r1` and `R_Send_r1` raise two new actions, read only by the new lemma. A lemma's "unless" names the compromises it excuses. Most excuse exactly what breaks the claim; a few excuse more, where Tamarin needs it to finish (assumption 11), or where the stronger claim wasn't tried: `heal_keys_secret` and `m4_heals_after_a_reply_the_attacker_only_watched` excuse x_B stolen before the first reply, which their guard on the responder's own T₂ makes needless. Anything a lemma doesn't name, the attacker may do at any time: hold every other key, and read, drop, reorder, replay and forge every message, as the relay can (§11).

**§1.1:**
- **x_B (the reader's key)** opens the first messages (`m1_secret` names it, and `m1_open_to_a_later_reader_key` finds it working after the reply). Stolen before the first reply reaches Alice, it takes the conversation over from her side (`reader_key_before_the_reply_takes_over`). Stolen later, it opens nothing more: `m3_forward_secret`, `m4_forward_secret`, `r1_forward_secret`, `r2_forward_secret`. Stolen early by an attacker who only watches, it still opens nothing Alice sent after Bob's own reply: `m3_secret_after_a_genuine_reply`, keyed on that reply's ratchet key.
- **x_A (the writer's key)** opens nothing: no secrecy lemma names it.
- **A stolen state,** either side's, taken after its first reply, opens messages until the next reply the attacker didn't write (`initiator_state_opens_m3_and_r2`, `responder_state_opens_m3`). After that each side's next message is secret again: `m4_heals_after_a_reply_the_attacker_only_watched`, and `r2_heals_after_the_responder_state` with `-D=RESPONDER_STATE`.
- **One message key,** a skipped key the reader keeps for a message still to come, opens that message and not the next on its chain (`skipped_key_opens_only_its_message`, `skipped_key_stolen_opens_its_message`). Alone it writes nothing here, since the tag's associated data holds w₀ (`skipped_agreement`); on the wire that rests on the outer layer (assumption 2), which envelope.spthy checks (`kept_key_alone_writes_nothing`). With w₀ too, which x_B gives even stolen after the reader accepted, a stolen state, or x_A once the responder has sent a continuation (assumption 13), it writes that message while the reader still waits for it (`skipped_key_and_reader_key_forge_its_message`). Each of §1.1's rows holds alone; this is two of them together.
- **Key-compromise impersonation:** the reader's own key lets anyone pose to it as any R_A (`reader_key_lets_anyone_pose_to_the_reader`), as a continuation of any of its conversations, which it labels "Continues" (`reader_key_forges_continues`), and as the owner of anyone's Seedelf in a reveal (`reader_key_forges_a_reveal`).

**§3 to §6:**
- **The static DH holds R_A:** a reader accepts a session whose A is P's key only if P started it, with the same cid, R_A, R_B and F (`static_dh_holds_r_a`). So no unknown-key share, and no replies sent to a mailbox the writer doesn't hold.
- **Both sides agree** on cid, R_A, R_B and every message (`responder_agreement_m1` to `_m4`, `initiator_agreement_r1` and `_r2`). A key stolen once the session runs can't forge into it: only a theft before the reader's first step excuses a later message, or a stolen state, which §1.1 says lets the attacker step in, and only one stolen before the message is read.
- **Continuations:** the static DH holds R_A with continuations about too: a reader accepts a first message whose A is P's key, fresh or a continuation, only if P sent it, with that cid, R_A, R_B and F, and reads only what P sent (`static_dh_holds_r_a_with_continuations`, `responder_agreement_m1_with_continuations`). So "Continues" shows only for a continuation the old other side started, unless its key or the reader's own was stolen first (`continues_only_from_the_old_other_side`), and it shows both ways. A continuation's message is secret unless the old other side's key is stolen (`continuation_message_secret`).
- **§5's rule on a cid is needed:** without it, a first message is read twice (`first_message_replays_without_the_cid_rule`).
- **Out of order** (§4.1): a message read after a later one on its chain, by the key kept for it, is read only as sent, and once (`skipped_agreement`, `skipped_each_read_once`), and both orders run (`skipped_in_order`, `skipped_out_of_order`).

**§19:**
- **The verified reveal names only its owner:** a reader shows "Verified ⟨Seedelf⟩" only if the Seedelf's owner revealed it in this conversation, which it started with this reader, unless the owner's key or the reader's was stolen first (`reveal_only_from_the_seedelfs_owner`). The proof's binding to cid, both identities, the name and the register is what holds it: an honest reveal can't be moved to another conversation, reader or name, a name the attacker minted with a copy of the register included. The reader's own key makes one for anyone (`reader_key_forges_a_reveal`), as §19.5 says.

Call 16 changed this model's rules and lemmas; check.sh is to prove each again, with nothing to warn. Before call 16 it proved the lemmas then here (2026-11-22).

- **The bare envelope** is construction.md §4.1 and §4.2: anyone seals to a fresh re-randomization of a handle, and the reader scans (the tag of [x]p is the header's), then opens with [x]c. Since call 16 the header carries a tag of w, never w, so no envelope holds a register of anyone's key (protocol.md §2, §9). The tag is a free function here: one-way, as SHA-256 is taken to be.
- **Every point the reader's key touches, the reader's side** (calls 13 and 16): every principal also has a second handle (g₂, [x]g₂), g₂ = [d]G for a d no one learns. A writer names the root handle and aims one point of its first message at g₂: the envelope's generator p, its ephemeral point c, or F's p₀, E_A, A or S_A, with the key that point needs taken from [k]v₂. The rules before call 16 (the outer check alone) took a message whose c, E_A, A or S_A was on g₂; only p was bound. Now each point is bound before the reader reacts: p by the outer check ([o]p₀), c by the ephemeral check ([r]p, r from the message key and the salt), p₀ by the base proof, E_A and S_A by the keys proof, and A by the keys proof's first branch. So every point its key touched is a power of the named handle's generator whose log the writer knows, and the reader's key on it is that power of the handle's public value, which the writer can compute anyway. Since call 16 both proofs are designated-verifier: each has a second branch made with the reader's own key, which proves any base, and any A. So for every point the one excuse is the reader's key stolen first, whose holder tests [x]g₂ = v₂ outright, no reaction needed; a trace lemma shows each branch doing it. Each of the four checks is shown needed by a control that drops it alone and finds the link with no key stolen.
- **The same, the initiator's side:** the responder writes the reply, so the attacker playing it holds every chain key. It aims a reply's p or c at the initiator's root handle, [k]G for a k it knows, or at its second. Without the outer check, or without the ephemeral check, the initiator takes it, and the responder learns who wrote. With both, p is [o]p₀ and c is [r][o]p₀ for the initiator's own base [b]G, which no k the attacker knows matches without b. (b is fresh here; the protocol derives it under x_A, §16.2, which only keeps it secret when the random source fails too.)
- **Bob's replies, sealed to R_A** (the session model's assumption 2). With x_B stolen before Bob reads the first message, the attacker writes that message itself, Bob's ratchet runs on the attacker's key, and only the seal to R_A hides his replies. This configuration checks that seal in the worst case: a sender seals to a principal's identity on an honest base or the attacker's, the attacker is given the message key, and the sealed content stands for the whole inner layer, the salt included. Since call 16 the envelope's ephemeral scalar r comes from the message key and the salt, as o does, so the attacker who held both would open the envelope: it's the salt, inside the envelope, that keeps the seal, as the drawn r did before. It stays secret unless that principal's key is stolen. With the session model's `r1_forward_secret`, which says what opens the inner layer, opening a reply takes x_A as well: argued from the two, not proved in one model.
- **A kept key, on the wire** (the session model's assumption 2). There, the tag needs w₀, which the session model's attacker lacks. On the wire, the key, the salt and one genuine ciphertext give the outer scalar o and the ephemeral scalar r, then the envelope's own plaintext, and AES-GCM redoes a tag without the associated data (-D=TAG_REDO). Before call 16 they gave w₀ too, from the envelope's register [o]w₀; since call 16 the header carries only a tag of [o]w₀, and w₀ is on the wire nowhere. This configuration gives the attacker exactly those means, so the claim no longer rests on the model leaving them out. It holds because the salt, the ciphertext and the base are inside envelopes only x_B opens, and w₀ is in none: a chain message's envelope key is [r]w with r from the kept key and the salt, so the salt, inside, is what keeps the kept key out. The kept key doesn't even open its message alone, which is more than §1.1's row claims. That x_B and the key together write it isn't shown here: the forgery is short (x_B opens the genuine envelope; the key and the tag's share make another under the same associated data, to the same register), but Tamarin's search for it ran out of 6 GB, named as far as a lemma can name it. The session model's `skipped_key_and_reader_key_forge_its_message` shows it without the envelope, and x_B opens every envelope the forgery needs. Nor is a continuation here: the responder's continuation hands the conversation's base, cid and w₀ to whoever holds the old initiator's key, so x_A and a kept key write the kept message too (assumption 13 of the session model).

**What it assumes,** beyond the session model's list, which holds where its rules are copied (assumptions 1, 3 to 5 and 12):

1. **A second handle stands for every other register of the key** whose generator the attacker knows no log of, for every point the reader's key touches. A sum of two handles is one; Tamarin's group has no addition, so sums themselves aren't written. The reader's conversations are on its root handle, and the second is "the other one", by symmetry. The wallet takes first messages only at a Seedelf it put under its key itself, or one the user turned on: a register someone else planted under the reader's key, a sum of two of its registers among them, that the user turns on makes every reply to a message written to it link what it sums, which no lemma here can show.
2. **The ratchet's AES-GCM is the wire's:** with the key, anyone reads a ciphertext and, from one genuine tag, makes another under the same associated data without knowing it (GHASH is linear); `tag_redone_without_the_associated_data` shows the equation does it. The session model's attacker can't; this one can. The power is re-tagging under the same associated data. A real key holder can also shift the associated data by a known difference, and with tags under three keys solve for a three-block unknown such as w₀ (joint-security.md, rule 1); neither is modelled, and neither is in reach of the attackers here, who hold one kept key and no genuine ciphertext (`-D=KEPT_KEY`), or every chain key and w₀ already (`-D=INITIATOR_READS`).
3. **The envelope's own AES-GCM** is the session model's: its associated data is the header, which is public, so anyone with its key makes any envelope under it anyway. The header's tag of w is a free function, one-way and equal only for equal points: SHA-256's collision resistance, taken as given.
4. **A configuration per claim, not the whole session sealed.** First contact, the reply and the second chain all sealed didn't finish Tamarin's precomputation in 20 GB (*How it's built*). So each claim keeps the part of the session it's about, and gives the attacker the rest:
   - `-D=SEALED_TO_A_KEY`: the reply's message key, as if the attacker held every key of the inner layer but x_A; not its salt, which the inner layer carries inside the envelope, and from which the outer and ephemeral scalars come (§16.2, call 16). The bases are an honest initiator's, with every principal's identity on them published, as F publishes it to anyone who opens it, or G^k (the session model's assumption 4).
   - `-D=INITIATOR_READS`: everything the responder knows, which here is the attacker: the base, A, the cid, and every chain key and w₀ it likes. A reply from an honest responder is the case where the attacker knows less.
   - `-D=KEPT_KEY`: one conversation as the session model leaves it at the initiator's second chain. Its chain keys are fresh, as the session model proves them secret, and its envelopes are the ones the conversation sends: the first message (F, without its base and keys proofs, which a kept key doesn't reach) and the chain's two messages to R_B, the reply (cid, T₁) to R_A. So the cases where the session model excuses the key, a theft before the session or a stolen state, aren't here: this configuration claims only that the kept key adds nothing on the wire. Nor is a continuation from the responder, which x_A opens (the session model's assumption 13).
   - `-D=FIRST` and its four controls: first contact alone, every principal writing and reading, each control without one of the reader's four checks. One first-contact message a conversation: a request's later messages, and the rule that each carries the first's header key S_A (`accept_with`, the join), aren't here; the crate's tests hold that rule.
5. **F's two proofs, by what they show** (call 16, protocol.md §3). Each is designated-verifier: it shows its writer knows the logs it claims, or the reader's own key.
   - **The keys proof.** An honest writer's is a fact, `!Proof(F, S_A, w₀)`, which `I_Start` leaves and the reader looks up (`R_Keys_proof_honest`): it binds F, S_A and that reader's w₀, as the proof's challenge does, so it serves that reader for that F alone. The attacker proves with exponents it names, sent beside the message (`R_Keys_proof_attacker`), and the checks make E_A, S_A and A those powers of p₀; or, once it holds the reader's key, with exponents for E_A and S_A and that key, sent the same way, and any A (`R_Keys_proof_by_the_reader_key`): the designated-verifier branch. In Tamarin's group the attacker raises only points it holds to exponents it knows, with no addition, so it proves here no more than a real prover can (and, by the next bullet, less, with no attack lost for the lemmas here).
   - **The attacker's exponents are public names** ($e, $s, $a), as its bases are G^$k (the session model's assumption 3), not any term it knows: sent as messages, three powers of one received point by them never finished Tamarin's variant computation (*How it's built*). No attack is lost for the lemmas here. Until the reader's key is out, no exponent the attacker knows holds the log of a second handle's generator, so no power of p₀ by one lies on that handle, a name or not; once it is out, `responder_takes_nothing_aimed_at_its_other_handle` excuses the take, and the designated-verifier branch takes any A anyway. The controls and traces need only some exponent, and a name is one.
   - **Why not a proof term** made with the exponents, as the reveal's is (the session model's assumption 14): the reader would match exponents it can't derive, which Tamarin's message-derivation check refuses as unintended pattern matching, and check.sh fails on its warning. The reveal's term passes only because its witness is a principal's key, which the reader's rule takes from that principal's fact; a writer's ephemeral exponents are in no fact.
   - **The base proof.** p₀ is an honest writer's base or G^k for a k the attacker names, both known powers of the named handle's generator (G here, the session model's assumption 4); or any point at all, which passes only once the reader's key is out (`R_Derive_m1a_any_base` and `base_check`): the designated-verifier branch, which proves any base.
   - **Not modelled:** either proof's soundness (special soundness of each Schnorr part and of the OR), its zero knowledge and deniability (the reader could have made it), and its nonce, whose safety for x is an algebraic fact the symbolic group can't state: z = k + c·x needs addition of exponents (joint-security.md, use 10 and rule 2; the crate's tests). On the wire F holds both proofs and SK binds them; here they travel beside F, which binds less and gives the attacker more.
6. **Not here:** padding and sizes (§9), the first-contact stamp (the session model's assumption 12), continuations (session.spthy's `-D=CONTINUATIONS` has them without the envelope; there A and p₀ are held values, and E′ and S′ are bound by the continuation's keys proof, argued from `-D=FIRST`, not checked), and timing: how long a reader takes on a message, which a far-ahead message's walk (§16.4, MAX_AUTH_STEPS) or a scan's early return could show, is outside a symbolic model.

## Unlinkability

[unlinkability.spthy](unlinkability.spthy) proves security.md's property 3 for the bare v1 envelope, as observational equivalence: Tamarin's diff terms, proved with `--diff`. Every envelope's header carries p, c and a tag of w (call 16), as on the wire.

| Run | Systems told apart | Must be | Before call 16, with w in the header (2026-11-22) |
|---|---|---|---|
| `same_or_two` | envelopes to one recipient, against envelopes to one recipient and to another | verified: no attacker tells them apart | verified (10,699 steps) |
| `known_or_no_one` (`-D=FRESH`) | envelopes to a known register, against envelopes to a key no one holds | verified | verified (10,602 steps) |
| `bare_register_links` (`-D=BARE_REGISTER`) | the same as the first, sealed to the register itself, not a re-randomization of it, as ElfTalk's messages carried their recipient's key | falsified: the link found | falsified (8 steps) |

The controls must find their link, so the model can see one; check.sh runs them all, and fails if a control is "verified". All seven are to be run with the tag. Before call 16 the first four took under a minute and a GB each.

**What it shows:** an attacker who holds both recipients' registers, of any number of pairs, chooses every plaintext, and sees any number of envelopes of either kind can't tell whether two envelopes go to the same recipient, or whether one goes to a given register at all. Diff-equivalence is stronger than observational equivalence, so "verified" is sound.

**A reader that answers** (`-D=ANSWERS`, calls 13 and 16): the attacker writes first messages to the reader's root handle, with any point aimed anywhere, and sees which the reader answers. If the answers differ when a second register is the reader's from when it's a key's no one holds, a reaction links the two. Diff-equivalence asks both sides to take the same steps, so the reader computes its key on each point in steps with no check (both sides take them alike) and checks everything in the one step that answers: the scan, the open, the inner layer under a key from [y]E with w₀ in its associated data, the outer check, the ephemeral check and the keys proof. E stands for F's E_A, A and S_A, which the keys proof binds alike, and the exponent the proof shows its writer knows is sent beside the message, any term it knows (envelope assumption 5 says why it's sent; envelope.spthy's three are public names). The base is G^k for a k the attacker names; neither proof's designated-verifier branch is here, since no key is stolen. It holds of the wallet only if nothing before that step is seen: no count of envelopes that opened but didn't read, no chain read set off by an envelope, no refusal acted on but an authenticated far-ahead one, and no page asked for at a time that depends on what the last page held (the worker's rules for Seedelf Messaging, architecture.md). Each control drops one check and must find the link.

**What it doesn't:**
- **A computational proof.** In the symbolic model the attacker computes only what the equations allow, so DDH holds by construction. The XDH argument with the per-envelope base is still to be written (security.md, *Before production*).
- **Metadata:** timing, size, and what the relay sees of posts and downloads (protocol.md §11). v1's envelope is 145 bytes plus the plaintext (161 before call 16's tag); session envelopes are padded to four sizes (§9).
- **The sender,** who knows b. **A reader's reaction** only as `-D=ANSWERS` has it: one reader, first messages, F beside the envelope, no reply, no continuation and no timing. A reaction to an envelope is an oracle (security.md 8); what limits the session layer's reactions is calls 13 and 16's checks (*The outer envelope*).
- **Sums of registers and a public G2 multiple of the key:** the model has no point addition and no pairing.
- **Session envelopes:** each goes to ([o]p₀, [o]w₀) with p₀ = [b]g_S, so to an observer without the keys it's v1's re-randomization of the handle by o·b, a scalar from secrets it doesn't have. Argued, not checked.

## What building and reviewing it found

A reviewer with no context read the model against protocol.md (2026-11-21). Its findings changed both.

- **w₀ in the associated data is load-bearing.** A writer can choose A, E_A and S_A on the handle's generator and compute SK on any base, so only the tag, which needs w₀ = [x_B]p₀, ties a first message to a base its writer can use. Without it, a reader's continuation would publish [x_B]p₀ for a base the attacker chose: a static-DH oracle on the spending key. protocol.md §3 now says so. Call 13's base proof (54a′) later added a second guard on the same point.
- **A stolen x_B, before Bob's first reply reaches Alice,** takes the conversation over from her side; and the reader's own key lets anyone pose to it, "Continues" included. Both are in protocol.md §1.1 and §7 now.
- **A request the bounds push out leaves no tombstone** (§16.4, a [choice]). So after a flood of 50 requests, a re-post of a first message, with new proof of work after the relay's 36 hours and within t + 48 hours, reads again as a new request. It changes no conversation; the reader sees a request twice. Tombstoning evicted requests would close it, at the cost of a tombstone per evicted request. It's the owner's call (chunk 54's plan).
- **The `[reuse]` lemmas first helped nothing** (2026-11-22). Their guard, `K(k)`, never meets a key (*How it's built*), so no later proof could close on one; and each one split the search on every message the attacker sent. With nine in view, most of the later lemmas ran out of an hour or of 12.5 GB. Guarded on the key, each lemma takes minutes. The claims didn't change: a guard on the attacker deriving a key says at least as much as one on its sending it.
- **Four continuation lemmas stood on an undated helper** (2026-11-22). Their static DH lemma said the secret is out only if one of the two keys was stolen, not that the theft came first. A proof that the reader accepted only P's own message then couldn't close the branch where a key is stolen after the forgery, and searched on past an hour. Dated, as the sessions' `static_dh_secret` always was, the helper proves in the same 79 steps, and three of the four in under 9 minutes each. The fourth, the reader's key forging "Continues", is a trace, and its search held 7 GB within 15 minutes until it named its forgery (*How it's built*). None of the four was false.
- **m4's agreement first excused the initiator's state stolen at any time** (2026-11-22, #405), as `responder_keys4_secret` stated it. Dated all the way down, as the claim needs, its `responder_keys2_secret` took 94 minutes and 6.5 GB of live heap; at 8 GB, it ran into the crawl below and looked out of time.
- **A run near its memory cap crawls instead of failing** (2026-11-22). With the heap capped, GHC compacts the oldest generation in place once live data passes 30% of the cap, and compacting is slow: major collections took 15 to 23 s at 3 to 4 GB, against 2 s copying at 2.4 GB, and one at 5 GB hadn't finished after 12 minutes at full CPU. So a run out of memory looked like a run out of time. check.sh now says why each run without a result stopped, and its peak live heap.
- **Two lemmas were first stated false.** `r1_forward_secret` and `r2_forward_secret` excused a theft of Bob's key only before he accepted Alice's own F. With Bob's key, the attacker writes another first message with her cid, p₀, A and w₀ ([x_B]A and w₀ are its to compute), Bob accepts it and replies under keys it knows. That's §1.1's key-compromise impersonation, which `reader_key_lets_anyone_pose_to_the_reader` shows. Their excuse now names a theft before Bob accepted any first message, as their claim in this README always said. Found while proving them (2026-11-22), from where the search went.
- **A stolen message key writes nothing alone** (2026-11-22). Its trace lemma was first stated the other way, that a kept skipped key lets its thief write the message it stands for, as in the Double Ratchet with public associated data. Tamarin found no such trace: the tag's associated data holds w₀, so the key alone can't make a tag the reader takes. Stated next without any excuse for the key, the agreement was false the other way: x_B stolen after the reader accepted gives w₀ = [x_B]p₀, and with the kept key the attacker writes that message. `skipped_agreement` now names exactly that pair. A fresh-context review then showed the key-alone half is the model's, not the wire's: once the inner layer is visible, the key and its salt give the envelope's outer scalar o, and [o]w₀ gives w₀, and AES-GCM's tag can be redone without the associated data. On the wire it rests on the outer layer (assumption 2), and envelope.spthy checks it there, with those means given to the attacker (*The outer envelope*). Since call 16 the header carries only a tag of [o]w₀, so that route to w₀ is gone too.
- **The two "takes nothing aimed at" lemmas watched only p** (the gate of 1.10.0, 2026-11-22). They named the envelope's register alone, while the reader's key also touches the envelope's ephemeral point c, F's p₀, E_A and A, and the first ratchet key. In this model as it was, with the outer check on, a reader took a first message whose c was a known multiple of its second handle's generator, its outer key from that handle's public value, and the lemma stayed verified; the same with E_A, A or S_A; and the initiator took a reply whose c was on its root handle. The crate did the same. Call 16 bound each point (the ephemeral scalar from the message key, and F's keys proof), and the lemmas now name every point, each with a control that finds the link without its check. Call 16 also made the base proof designated-verifier, whose second branch, made with the reader's key, proves any base: so the reader's own key, stolen first, became the one excuse for every point, where the lemma had none, and a trace lemma shows it's needed.
- **The model's own first version** let a stolen state open nothing: the attacker decrypted only with the associated data, and no state reveal carried w₀, its one part not on the wire. Decryption is key-only now, as AES-GCM allows, and each state reveal carries w₀.

## What the model assumes

Where a symbolic model is weaker than the protocol, or this one narrower. A reviewer should read these first.

1. **Perfect cryptography.** Tamarin's attacker controls the network and computes only what the equations allow: Diffie–Hellman in a prime-order group, where [a]P and [b]P never give [ab]P; AES-GCM, read with the key alone and accepted only under its key and associated data; HKDF and HMAC as one-way functions. Nothing about bounds, timing or side channels.
2. **No outer envelope.** The attacker sees every inner message, F, cid and headers included. That gives it more, since the outer layer authenticates nothing: anyone can seal to a register. But what the outer layer shows goes too: the envelope's generator [o]p₀, its ephemeral point [r][o]p₀ and a tag of [o]w₀ (call 16), whose o and r come from the message key and the inner layer's salt (§16.2). Two claims rest on the outer layer. They go unchecked here, and [envelope.spthy](envelope.spthy) checks each, in a configuration of its own (*The outer envelope*):
   - **§1.1's "x_B opens nothing Bob sent"** when x_B is stolen before Bob reads the first message. Bob's replies are then keyed to the attacker's ratchet key, and only their seal to R_A (x_A) hides them. So `r1_forward_secret` excuses a theft before Bob accepts. Checked as `replies_sealed_to_r_a`.
   - **A kept message key, stolen alone, writes nothing** (`skipped_agreement`). Here its tag needs w₀, which the attacker lacks. On the wire, whoever sees the inner layer gets o and r from the key and the salt, and AES-GCM's tag can be redone without the associated data from one genuine ciphertext and the key; before call 16 the envelope's [o]w₀ gave w₀ too, and since, its header carries only a tag of it. What keeps a key thief from the salt and the ciphertext is the outer layer, which only x_B or the device opens, and either gives w₀ anyway. Found by the follow-up's review; checked, with those means given to the attacker, as `kept_key_alone_writes_nothing`.
3. **Handles are root registers,** (G, [x]G), one per principal. The attacker can compute [x]P for any P it knows the discrete log of, as it can over a Seedelf datum's own generator. Several registers of one key, and their sums, aren't modelled here (envelope.spthy's second handle stands for them); protocol.md §2 states the link that sums give. The writer's read of a handle and the reader's read of the chain are honest facts (`!Pk`, `!Chain`): the trusted-source limit protocol.md §2 and §19.4 state. The attacker's base is G^$k, $k a public name it chooses, not any term it knows.
4. **The base a reader accepts is an honest writer's p₀, or G^k for a k the attacker chose.** It was the model's main restriction; since calls 12 and 13 (54a′), the reader enforces it.
   - **Why it's there.** The reader publishes [t]p₀, its ratchet key, on whatever base the writer chose. With p₀ an arbitrary term, Tamarin's search never closes.
   - **Why nothing is lost.** Two rules tie a first message to a base its writer can use (protocol.md §3). F's base proof shows its writer knows b with p₀ = [b]g_S for the handle named in `to`, or knows the reader's own key: since call 16 it's designated-verifier, so it convinces no one but that reader. Without that key, over the model's one generator G, the base is G^k. And the tag's associated data holds w₀ = [x_B]p₀, which the attacker knows, without x_B, for G^k through the handle, and for an honest conversation's base once w₀ is out (a stolen state, or a continuation). A continuation carries no proof, but must be on one of the reader's own conversations' bases (§6). All of these are in the model.
   - **With x_B,** the base proof is made for any base, which this model doesn't write. Whoever holds x_B already computes every value the reader computes, on any base; what the reader adds on such a base, its own fresh ratchet keys, stays in the conversation the attacker opened, and no other conversation's keys depend on it. So no lemma here gains a trace from it, including the ones that don't excuse x_B, such as `m3_secret_after_a_genuine_reply`. That's argued, not checked. envelope.spthy's `-D=FIRST` writes that branch, for the claim it's there for (`reader_key_proves_a_base_on_its_other_handle`).
   - **What's left out:** a base proved over another register of x_B than the root one (3). Its writer knows b over that register's generator, so it gains what a G^k session gives. That's argued, not checked. The proof's soundness, its deniability and its nonce are the crate's tests' (54a′, call 16), not the model's.
5. **Every point is valid.** The symbolic group has no identity and no small subgroup, so §3's point checks are the code's and the tests' (54a), not the model's.
6. **A bounded conversation:** one first-contact message, two full round trips (m1, r1, m3, r2, m4), and one continuation link (a continuation isn't continued again). Out of order is one chain of two messages read either way (`-D=SKIPPED`), with no later step after it. MAX_SKIP, the requests' bounds and each request's share (16 messages and 64 KiB, call 16), every clock (the 48-hour window, a skipped key's 48 hours, tombstones, expiry) and proof of work aren't here: they're counters and times, which the tests cover. So is the far-ahead verdict, the one refusal the wallet acts on: since call 16 it's given only for a message that authenticated and passed its envelope's checks, on a read-only walk of at most MAX_AUTH_STEPS keys (§16.4), so it comes after every check, as the model's reads do. The mutation passes (security.md) predate call 16's rules.
7. **§5's rules are one restriction:** a reader takes a given cid's first message once. That's what the cid table, the tombstones and the window aim at, and the model shows it's needed. The code keeps it except where no tombstone is left while the window is open: a request the bounds push out (above; since call 16 only a new request pushes one out, never a join); a conversation deleted when its record can't be read, its F and so its t lost; one the sweep unlists because its record is missing; and a wallet restored from the phrase, or the phrase's second browser, which never held the cid. In each, a first message still on the relay and inside its window reads again as a new request, and in the last two a reply to it reaches nobody (protocol.md §6).
8. **Requests are read eagerly.** 54a's tests show replying builds exactly the state eager processing would (§16.4), for the messages a request holds. Since call 16 a request also refuses what eager processing would read: a join past its share, or past the 256 KiB of all requests, since a join never pushes another out. One first-contact message never meets either.
9. **Reading is two rules,** deriving the keys from the header, then authenticating. A message that fails leaves the model's state stuck where the code leaves it unchanged, which removes only traces in which the failure changed nothing.
10. **ENCRYPT's salt is left out.** A message key is used once in the model, and the salt is a net for a key used twice (§4.2), which only a rollback does. The model has no rollback. Since call 16 the salt is a send's one fresh value: the envelope's o and r both come from it and the message key, so a key used twice with a salt repeated gives two inner messages one outer key and nonce, where a drawn r used to keep them apart. envelope.spthy has the salt; neither model has the rollback.
11. **State theft:** each side's record after its first reply, taken once, then or later, which also stands for an old copy: the initiator's in the sessions' configuration, the responder's in its own (13). Not modelled: both at once, and a state before the first reply. The responder's record here lacks its receiving chain key, which the stored state holds; with one first-contact message it would open only the initiator's later first-contact messages, which the model doesn't send. Of §1.1's "messages before the copy", m1 is proved against the initiator's copy and r1 excused there; against the responder's copy neither is stated, and both hold by what the copy holds (the next key of each chain, never the one used). One message key alone is a skipped key the reader keeps, taken once (`-D=SKIPPED`).
    - **Some lemmas name the initiator's state where it opens nothing:** `responder_keys2_secret` and `r1_forward_secret`. Tamarin finishes them only that way; without the excuse it has to refute every branch the state would end, and ran past 12 GB. They still say everything about traces where no state is stolen, and `responder_keys2_secret` names only a state stolen before the attacker knew the key, which m4's agreement needs. That costs Tamarin an hour and a half and 6.5 GB of live heap, so check.sh gives that lemma its own room (the `// check.sh:` line above it).
12. **F is one value bound into SK, and through it into every key:** cid, t, `replaces`, p₀, A and E_A. `t` is any public value. `to`, its base proof (call 13, 54a′) and its keys proof (call 16) are left out. `to` changes only a label, and what the base proof gives the reader, a base its writer can use, is assumption 4. The keys proof only narrows what a reader accepts: here the attacker chooses E_A, A and S_A freely, where on the wire it must know their logs on p₀ (or hold the reader's key), so every lemma here holds with it too. A trace a lemma names may use points no writer could prove on the wire: `reader_key_forges_continues` names E = S = G on an honest base, whose log on it no one knows; on the wire the forger takes powers of p₀ it chose, and the trace is the same. envelope.spthy's `-D=FIRST` checks it, for the claim it's there for (no reaction links two handles). The base proof's designated-verifier branch is assumption 4's. The first-contact stamp (call 12) sits after F, and it and the envelope's outer and ephemeral scalars (§4.1) are about spam and unlinkability, which no lemma here claims (2, 6).
13. **Six configurations,** since Tamarin's search grows with every rule that publishes something, and with every compromise. Each lemma is proved where its own compromises are.
    - **The sessions** steal long-term keys and the initiator's state, and have no continuations. Their attacker misses what a continuation publishes: fresh powers of p₀, a message under a key it can't derive, and the old identities. Of those only w₀ isn't otherwise public, and a continuation is sealed to the other side, so only a holder of that side's key sees it. That holder gains something here: the responder's continuation carries p₀, the old cid and w₀ (its A) to R_A, so x_A, stolen at any time, and one kept key write the message the key stands for, the reader's key safe. `skipped_agreement`'s excuses leave that out, and so does `kept_key_alone_writes_nothing` (envelope.spthy); `-D=SKIPPED_CONTINUED`, if built, shows it.
    - **The responder's state** (`-D=RESPONDER_STATE`) is the sessions with the responder's state stolen instead of the initiator's. With both in one model, Tamarin ran past 14 GB on the third key; a lemma about one side's state doesn't need the other's.
    - **The continuations** (`-D=CONTINUATIONS`) have no ratchet and no state theft. Their attacker misses the ratchet's fresh powers of p₀ and its ciphertexts, which no continuation's SK depends on.
    - **The cid check** (`-D=NO_CID_RULE`) is the sessions without §5's rule.
    - **The reveal** (`-D=REVEAL`) is first contact, Seedelfs on chain and the reveal, with no ratchet: the reveal goes in the clear, where the protocol sends it inside the ratchet, so its attacker sees more than the real one. Its claim is about who could have made the proof, which needs no ratchet.
    - **Skipped keys** (`-D=SKIPPED`) are the sessions up to the first reply, then the initiator's next chain with two messages, and the kept key stolen; no later step and no state theft. Their helpers are the sessions' own, proved again there.
14. **The verified reveal's proof is two constructors** (`-D=REVEAL`, protocol.md §19): `prf1` the real branch, made with the key of A and of the Seedelf's register, `prf2` the other, made with w₀'s, each binding cid, p₀, A, w₀, the name and the register. The reader checks one by equality, as the real check's equations hold exactly then, with its witness a principal's key: any principal's for the real branch, its own for the other. A proof by a key no principal holds verifies only for registers of that key, so for no principal's Seedelf, and the lemma is about a principal's; the attacker still uses any key it steals. Not modelled:
    - **the proof's soundness,** which the abstraction assumes: the Cramer–Damgård–Schoenmakers argument, the crate's tests, a mutation pass and vectors reproduced from protocol.md alone (security.md, *The verified reveal*);
    - **its deniability,** that the reader could have made it, so it convinces no one else: a claim about what a third party can tell apart, which a trace lemma can't state (it would need Tamarin's observational equivalence);
    - **the chain read** (§19.4's step 4): a Seedelf's name holds one register for good here, so a Seedelf that moves owner, and the fresh read that catches it, are the wallet's and its tests';
    - **only after the first reply** (§19.1): the initiator reveals at any time after it starts, which gives the attacker more.

## How it's built

- **Rules,** following §16: `I_*` are the initiator's steps, `R_*` the responder's, `C_Derive` and `C_Send_*` a continuation by either side, and `R_Label_*` the reader's "Continues" match, judged when the request arrives (`judged_on_arrival`). `Seedelf` and `Seedelf_attacker` put names on chain, `I_Reveal` sends a reveal and `R_Check_reveal_*` check one; `I_Send_m3_first` and `_second` send a chain of two, and `R_Read_m3*` read it in either order, keeping a skipped key. `Reveal_ltk`, `Reveal_state_initiator`, `Reveal_state_responder` and `Reveal_skipped_key` are the compromises.
- **No step raises received points to a power on more than two bases,** and no step publishes a key it computed from a point it was given: reads are split into deriving the keys, then authenticating, and a continuation into deriving, then sending, once per kind of base. Tamarin's precomputation and search don't finish otherwise.
- **Each step's root key and chain key are proved secret one step at a time** (`keys1_secret` to `keys4_secret`, `heal_keys_secret`, `responder_keys2_secret` and `_keys4_secret`), and the initiator's agreement lemmas too. Each is a `[reuse]` lemma the later ones in its configuration assume, proved in its own run; check.sh runs them all. In the continuations, `static_dh_secret_with_continuations` and `static_dh_holds_r_a_with_continuations` do the same for the static DH and its hold on R_A.
- **A `[reuse]` lemma's excuse is dated** as the lemmas standing on it need: `static_dh_secret` and `_with_continuations` name a theft before the attacker knew the secret, so a proof that a message is forged only after a theft closes on them. Undated, they closed nothing that needed the order (*What building and reviewing it found*).
- **A keys lemma guards on the key itself,** `KU(rk)` and `KU(ck)`, the attacker deriving that key, so a later proof that reaches the key closes there. Two other guards fail, and the first version had both:
  - **`K(k)`** never fires: in a lemma, `K` is the attacker sending a message into the protocol (Tamarin's ISEND), and a key is never sent. It only split the search on every message the attacker sent.
  - **`KU(k)` with k = rk or ck** fires on every term the attacker derives, and the equality splits the search on each one.
  - **`keys4_secret` is the exception:** in the key-guarded form Tamarin didn't prove it in two hours at 12 GB, in the `KU(k)` form it takes minutes. So it's stated that way, `keys4_secret_guarded` restates it guarded on each key (proved from it in 21 steps), and the lemmas after see only the twin.
- **An all-traces lemma hides the `[reuse]` lemmas its proof doesn't need** (`hide_lemma`), so they cost it nothing; some, `m1_secret` among them, hide only the one that slowed them. Hiding a lemma only drops an assumption. **A trace lemma sees them all:** they cut every branch where the attacker would build a message it has no key for. The continuations' two "Continues" traces are the exception, hiding their helpers as they always have.
- **Each trace lemma names its trace step by step:** `executable` the whole conversation, message by message, and the others the honest exchange up to their theft; `reader_key_before_the_reply_takes_over` with no reply from Bob at all, and `reader_key_lets_anyone_pose_to_the_reader` on a base and a cid no honest initiator used. `reader_key_forges_continues` names its forgery too: x_B stolen after an honest conversation, then a continuation of it on the old base with E = S = G, which any point would do here (on the wire, since call 16, powers of p₀ the forger chose: assumption 12). Naming more only narrows what the lemma says exists, and it keeps Tamarin's search on that trace.
- **The responder's side stands on lemmas about the first message:** `static_dh_secret` and `static_dh_holds_r_a` for its agreement, and `responder_accepted_the_initiators_f` for its keys, since `responder_keys2_secret` and `_keys4_secret` name the initiator's own F. In the continuations, `static_dh_holds_r_a_with_continuations` carries both the agreement and the label.
- **A tactic, `states_then_keys`,** ranks the goals of the lemmas Tamarin's default ranking stalled on: the protocol's states first, so a `[reuse]` lemma has the rule instances it matches, then what the attacker knows of fresh values and of keys, Diffie–Hellman case splits late, and how the attacker built a message last. A ranking changes how long a proof takes, never what it proves.
- **`KeyOn(P, p₀, A)`** is an observer rule that publishes nothing. A lemma can't write "A = [x_P]p₀" with a power in its formula, so it says KeyOn.

**The envelope model** (2026-11-22) copies the session model's rules and seals each message. Its precomputation first ran out of memory, past 10 GB, and each cause took a bisection, rule by rule, to find:
- **A value the attacker supplied, sealed back under an encryption.** The reader's reply carries the cid it read. Inside an envelope, with the envelope's key, the attacker takes it back out as a value Tamarin can't place, and Tamarin's saturation of sources didn't end. Neither `--auto-sources` nor a sources lemma closed it; typing every cid as fresh did. No configuration here seals a received cid any more, so cids are general again.
- **A point the rule only matched, raised and published.** w₀ = [b]v_B on a v_B matched from `!Pk` is a power of a variable, and the envelopes publish powers of it: the same kind of chain. The initiator's rules take the reader's key from its fact instead, and use it only in w₀ (the comment above `I_Start`).
- **A product written in a rule.** `'g'^(~xr*~rb)` fails Tamarin's multiplication restriction, a soundness condition of its Diffie–Hellman theory, while `('g'^~xr)^~rb` is the same point and passes.
- **The scan's power in a pattern.** The tag of [x]p is checked against the header's by equality (`Eq`), as the reveal's checks are. The open keeps [x]c in its pattern, where the message-derivation check sees how the reader gets the key.
- **F's proofs by what they show** (call 16, envelope assumption 5): an honest writer's keys proof is a fact the reader looks up, and the attacker's exponents travel beside the message, never inside a proof term the reader matches. Tamarin's message-derivation check refuses a variable a rule only matches inside a constructor with no destructor ("Failed to derive Variable(s)", as an early version of the reveal's rules met it), and check.sh fails on the warning.
- **The attacker's keys-proof exponents are public names** (the gate of 1.10.0, envelope assumption 5). Sent as messages, `R_Keys_proof_attacker`'s three powers of one received point by three received exponents gave Maude's variant computation more than a thousand variants in its first minute, with no end: each `-D=FIRST` configuration's wellformedness checks ran out of 2,400 s at 13 MB, waiting on Maude after "Theory closed" (the query, logged through `--with-maude`, was that rule's). As names, as the attacker's bases are G^$k, the rule has 59 variants, the designated-verifier branch's 19, and each configuration's checks finish in about 15 s.
- **The responder's reads carry p₀, not its exponent,** so one chain of rules serves an honest base, G^k and the designated-verifier branch's any base. Nothing they derive is published (first contact's reply isn't in this model), so a base the rules only matched starts no chain of sources, where in the session model the reply publishes [t]p₀ (its assumption 4).
- **A configuration per claim.** Each sealed message multiplies the sources Tamarin saturates. First contact alone (`-D=FIRST`) is proved in minutes at 6 GB. First contact and the reply, with or without the initiator's reading of it, didn't finish saturating in 25 minutes at 8 GB, or in 20 GB, whatever the open's encoding. The claims that needed the reply and the second chain each got a configuration of their own (*The outer envelope*, assumption 4), proved in seconds to minutes under 1 GB.
- **An exponent the attacker sends, in a published power,** loops the search, as session.spthy's README found for bases: `-D=SEALED_TO_A_KEY` first took the outer scalar from the attacker and ran out of memory, and with a fresh scalar it publishes, proves in seconds. The attacker raises any point it holds to any power it knows, so a scalar given is as good as one chosen.
- **`[reuse]` helpers on what a forgery would need** (`kept_conversation_secret`, `kept_salt_secret`, dated as the lemma on them needs). With them, and the chain's counter typed, `kept_key_alone_writes_nothing` takes 3 minutes; before, it ran past 20.
- **AES-GCM's tag** is two constructors and an equation, `share(tagc(s, k, m), k, m) = s`: a rule that pulled the associated data out of a ciphertext instead failed the derivation check.

## Why Tamarin and not ProVerif

The plan named ProVerif by default. ProVerif takes Diffie–Hellman as an equation, and the registers need it with any point as the base: p₀ is itself a power of G, and w₀, E_A and the DH outputs are powers of p₀. Its completion of `exp(exp(P, x), y) = exp(exp(P, y), x)` didn't finish on a one-line model. Tamarin's built-in theory takes any base.
